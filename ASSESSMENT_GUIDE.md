# Percepto Assessment Guide

**For Clinicians, Therapists, and Educators**

---

## Overview

This guide explains each assessment category in Percepto, what skills they evaluate, why those skills matter clinically, what difficulties may be observed, and how results should be interpreted.

**Clinical Disclaimer:**
> Percepto is a visual perception screening and profiling aid. It is not a diagnostic device. Results should be interpreted by a qualified clinician in the context of a comprehensive evaluation.

---

## 1. Visual Discrimination

### What It Evaluates
The ability to identify similarities and differences between visual stimuli including shapes, colors, sizes, patterns, and emotional expressions.

### Why It Matters
Visual discrimination is foundational for:
- **Reading:** Distinguishing between similar letters (b/d, p/q) and words
- **Mathematics:** Recognizing number forms and mathematical symbols
- **Daily Living:** Matching clothes, finding objects, recognizing signs
- **Social Interaction:** Reading facial expressions and emotional cues

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Confusing similar letters or numbers
- Difficulty matching socks or similar objects
- Trouble distinguishing colors, especially similar tones
- Challenges recognizing facial expressions
- Slow or inaccurate visual scanning
- Frequent errors in copying from board to paper

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Accurate matching of similar but not identical stimuli
- Quick identification of differences
- Consistent performance across trials

**Areas of Concern:**
- High error rate on matching tasks
- Need for extended time to make discriminations
- Consistent confusion of specific stimulus pairs
- Better performance with high-contrast vs. low-contrast stimuli

**Intervention Implications:**
- May benefit from high-contrast materials
- Color-coding strategies may help
- Larger print or magnified materials
- Direct instruction in letter/number discrimination

## 2. Visual Closure

### What It Evaluates
The ability to recognize objects, forms, or patterns when they are incomplete, partially hidden, or viewed from unusual angles.

### Why It Matters
Visual closure supports:
- **Reading:** Recognizing words when letters are obscured or handwritten
- **Object Recognition:** Identifying items in clutter or poor lighting
- **Academic Skills:** Understanding incomplete diagrams or partial information
- **Daily Living:** Finding partially hidden objects, reading signs from distance

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Cannot recognize objects unless fully visible
- Difficulty with "fill in the blank" activities
- Struggles with handwriting recognition
- Trouble identifying objects in photographs with occlusion
- Needs to see entire object to name it

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Recognizes common objects with 50-75% visibility
- Completes partial patterns accurately
- Understands obscured text through context

**Areas of Concern:**
- Requires nearly complete visibility to recognize objects
- Cannot complete partially drawn shapes
- Significant difficulty with visually degraded stimuli
- Better with 3D objects than 2D representations

**Intervention Implications:**
- Provide complete, unambiguous visual information
- Reduce visual clutter in learning materials
- Use real objects when possible rather than pictures
- Explicit teaching of pattern completion strategies

## 3. Visual Form Constancy

### What It Evaluates
The ability to recognize that a form or object is the same regardless of changes in size, color, orientation, position, or font/style.

### Why It Matters
Form constancy enables:
- **Reading:** Recognizing letters in different fonts, sizes, and handwriting styles
- **Mathematics:** Understanding that "5" is five regardless of presentation
- **Navigation:** Recognizing landmarks from different angles
- **Generalization:** Applying learned concepts to new presentations

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Only recognizes letters in specific fonts
- Cannot generalize number recognition across presentations
- Difficulty with rotated or flipped objects
- Treats same object at different distances as different objects
- Struggles with handwritten vs. printed text

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Recognizes forms regardless of presentation changes
- Generalizes across size variations
- Understands rotation and reflection

**Areas of Concern:**
- Recognition tied to specific presentation format
- Treats rotated objects as different objects
- Cannot recognize forms at different sizes
- Letter/number confusion increases with font variation

**Intervention Implications:**
- Teach explicitly using multiple font presentations
- Use consistent materials before introducing variation
- Label objects explicitly to support recognition
- Provide size/distance constancy training

## 4. Visual Memory

### What It Evaluates
The ability to remember and recall visual information after a short delay, including shapes, patterns, and spatial arrangements.

### Why It Matters
Visual memory supports:
- **Spelling:** Remembering visual patterns of words
- **Reading:** Word recognition and sight vocabulary
- **Note-Taking:** Copying from board or screen
- **Navigation:** Remembering routes and landmarks
- **Academic Skills:** Memorizing diagrams, charts, and visual sequences

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Cannot remember what was seen moments ago
- Difficulty copying from board to paper
- Poor spelling despite good phonetic skills
- Forgets faces, places, or visual instructions
- Needs frequent visual references
- Cannot play memory-based games

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Recalls 4-6 items after brief exposure
- Retains visual information for copying tasks
- Shows improvement with repeated exposure

**Areas of Concern:**
- Significantly reduced span of visual memory
- Cannot retain information long enough to copy
- No improvement with repetition
- Better with verbal than visual memory

**Intervention Implications:**
- Provide written/photographic references
- Use verbal descriptions to support visual memory
- Reduce memory load in tasks
- Allow extended time for visual processing
- Use multi-sensory approaches

---

## 5. Visual Sequential Memory

### What It Evaluates
The ability to remember and reproduce a sequence of visual stimuli in the correct order.

### Why It Matters
Sequential memory is essential for:
- **Daily Living Skills:** Dressing, grooming, hygiene routines
- **Following Instructions:** Multi-step directions in sequence
- **Academic Skills:** Procedure following, math operations, writing
- **Social Skills:** Understanding social sequences and routines
- **Executive Function:** Planning and organizing sequential tasks

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Cannot remember steps in order (dressing, morning routine)
- Reverses sequence of actions
- Misses steps in multi-step tasks
- Difficulty with routines and transitions
- Cannot replicate demonstrated sequences
- Poor procedural memory

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Reproduces 4-5 item sequences accurately
- Maintains temporal order
- Shows understanding of before/after relationships

**Areas of Concern:**
- Unable to recall sequence beyond 2-3 items
- Frequent order reversals
- Random rather than systematic approach
- Better with verbal than visual sequential cues

**Intervention Implications:**
- Provide visual schedules and checklists
- Use photograph sequences for routines
- Break tasks into smaller steps
- Teach self-monitoring strategies
- Establish consistent routines

## 6. Visual Spatial Skills

### What It Evaluates
The ability to understand spatial relationships, navigate space, judge distances, and perceive object positions relative to oneself and other objects.

### Why It Matters
Spatial skills support:
- **Navigation:** Moving through environment, map reading
- **Sports:** Judging distances, coordination, ball skills
- **Handwriting:** Spacing letters and words, staying on line
- **Mathematics:** Geometry, measurement, number line concepts
- **Daily Living:** Parking, reaching for objects, spatial planning

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Gets lost easily, even in familiar environments
- Poor handwriting spacing and alignment
- Clumsiness, bumping into things
- Difficulty with sports requiring spatial judgment
- Trouble with maps and directions
- Misjudges distances when reaching
- Poor organizational skills in physical space

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Accurate spatial localization
- Good navigation in familiar and novel spaces
- Appropriate distance judgment
- Understanding of left/right, up/down, in/out

**Areas of Concern:**
- Disorientation in space
- Poor understanding of spatial prepositions
- Difficulty with left/right discrimination
- Clumsiness and poor motor planning
- Significant trouble with navigation

**Intervention Implications:***
- Provide explicit spatial language instruction
- Use landmarks for navigation support
- Practice left/right discrimination
- Reduce spatial complexity in tasks
- Allow extra space for movement

## 7. Visual Neglect

### What It Evaluates
Awareness of and attention to the entire visual field, particularly both sides of space. Screens for hemispatial neglect and visual field deficits.

### Why It Matters
Visual attention assessment is critical for:
- **Post-Stroke Evaluation:** Detecting hemispatial neglect
- **Brain Injury:** Identifying attentional deficits
- **Reading:** Ensuring full word/line processing
- **Safety:** Awareness of full environment
- **Driving Readiness:** Complete visual field attention

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Ignores stimuli on one side of space
- Incomplete drawings or copying
- Bumping into objects on one side
- Reading only half of words or lines
- Poor awareness of personal space on affected side
- Difficulty finding objects in crowded arrays

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Equal attention to both sides of space
- Scans full visual field systematically
- No omissions on cancellation tasks
- Accurate line bisection

**Areas of Concern:**
- Consistent omissions on one side
- Systematic deviation on line bisection
- Incomplete drawings/copying
- Poor self-awareness of deficit
- Performance varies with cueing

**Intervention Implications:**
- Requires medical/neurological evaluation
- May benefit from prism adaptation therapy
- Anchor stimuli on affected side
- Use visual scanning training
- Adapt materials and environment

## 8. Figure Ground

### What It Evaluates
The ability to distinguish an object or target from its background, particularly when the background is complex or competing.

### Why It Matters
Figure-ground perception enables:
- **Reading:** Finding place on page, tracking lines
- **Visual Search:** Locating objects in cluttered environments
- **Academic Skills:** Worksheets with multiple elements
- **Daily Living:** Finding clothes in drawer, items in refrigerator
- **Attention:** Focusing on relevant visual information

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Cannot find objects in cluttered drawer or room
- Loses place when reading
- Overwhelmed by busy worksheets
- Cannot pick out relevant from irrelevant visual information
- Difficulty with "Where's Waldo" type activities
- Trouble reading text over patterned backgrounds

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Locates targets in moderate visual clutter
- Can focus on relevant visual information
- Maintains place when reading
- Adequate visual search strategies

**Areas of Concern:**
- Significant difficulty with any visual clutter
- Cannot locate targets in simple backgrounds
- Frequent loss of place in reading
- Visually overwhelmed by moderate complexity
- Poor visual search efficiency

**Intervention Implications:**
- Reduce visual clutter in materials
- Use high-contrast, uncluttered worksheets
- Provide reading guides or windows
- Teach systematic scanning strategies
- Organize physical environment to reduce clutter

## 9. Motion Perception

### What It Evaluates
The ability to detect, track, and interpret moving objects, including direction, speed, and trajectory.

### Why It Matters
Motion perception supports:
- **Sports:** Ball tracking, timing movements, coordination
- **Safety:** Judging approaching vehicles, crossing streets
- **Social Interaction:** Reading body language, gesture recognition
- **Driving:** Essential for safe vehicle operation
- **Academic:** Following moving demonstrations, video content

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Poor sports performance requiring ball tracking
- Difficulty crossing streets safely
- Cannot catch or intercept moving objects
- Trouble following moving demonstrations
- Avoids activities with moving elements
- Misjudges speed of approaching objects

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Accurate tracking of moving targets
- Appropriate judgment of motion direction and speed
- Can intercept moving objects
- Understands trajectory prediction

**Areas of Concern:**
- Cannot track smooth motion
- Poor judgment of speed/direction
- Difficulty with interception tasks
- Motion causes discomfort or avoidance
- Better with static than dynamic visual information

**Intervention Implications:**
- Provide static alternatives when possible
- Caution with activities requiring motion judgment
- May need support for street crossing
- Consider impact on sports participation
- Static demonstrations instead of moving

## 10. Pursuit & Fixation

### What It Evaluates
Control of eye movements including the ability to maintain steady fixation on a target and smoothly track moving objects.

### Why It Matters
Oculomotor control is essential for:
- **Reading:** Accurate eye movements across text
- **Attention:** Maintaining focus on tasks
- **Academic Skills:** Copying from board, sustained visual attention
- **Sports:** Eye-hand coordination, tracking
- **Daily Living:** Safe navigation, visual exploration

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Poor reading fluency despite good decoding
- Loses place frequently when reading
- Head movement when reading (instead of eye movement)
- Cannot maintain eye contact
- Difficulty with sustained visual attention
- Poor eye-hand coordination

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Stable fixation on targets
- Smooth pursuit eye movements
- Accurate saccades between targets
- Minimal head movement during reading

**Areas of Concern:**
- Nystagmus or unstable fixation
- Jerky pursuit movements
- Need for excessive head movement
- Poor sustained fixation
- Significant reading difficulty related to eye movements

**Intervention Implications:**
- Consider vision therapy evaluation
- May benefit from oculomotor exercises
- Use reading guides to support tracking
- Larger print to reduce saccade frequency
- Occupational therapy for eye-hand coordination

**Note:** The mirror.html assessment uses webcam-based eye tracking and provides screening-level information only. Professional oculomotor evaluation should supplement these results.

## 11. Observation Tasks

### What It Evaluates
Structured behavioral observation of visual behaviors in functional contexts, including environmental factors and compensatory strategies.

### Why It Matters
Behavioral observation provides:
- **Functional Context:** How vision impacts real-world activities
- **Compensatory Strategies:** What the individual has developed naturally
- **Environmental Factors:** How lighting, clutter, and setup affect performance
- **Holistic View:** Integration of visual skills in natural settings
- **Family Input:** Observer/parent perspectives on visual functioning

### Difficulties That May Be Observed
- Visual fatigue during sustained tasks
- Preference for specific lighting conditions
- Consistent head positions or postures
- Avoidance of visual tasks
- Need for physical guidance
- Difficulty with transitions or new environments

### Clinical Interpretation
**Typical Performance:**
- Efficient visual exploration
- Adapts to varying visual conditions
- Maintains attention appropriately for age
- Uses effective strategies

**Areas of Concern:**
- Significant visual fatigue
- Strong environmental preferences/aversions
- Limited visual exploration
- Heavy reliance on non-visual cues
- Clear patterns of visual difficulty

**Intervention Implications:**
- Environmental modifications based on observations
- Educational accommodations
- Compensatory strategy instruction
- Visual efficiency training
- Family education and support

## General Interpretation Guidelines

### Score Interpretation

Percepto assessments typically provide:
- **Accuracy Score:** Percentage of correct responses
- **Completion Time:** Time taken to finish assessment
- **Error Pattern:** Types of mistakes made

**Interpretation Framework:**

| Performance Level | Accuracy | Clinical Implication |
|-------------------|----------|---------------------|
| Excellent | 80-100% | Skill well-developed, may not need intervention |
| Good | 60-79% | Skill present but may benefit from support |
| Fair | 40-59% | Skill emerging, intervention likely helpful |
| Needs Support | Below 40% | Significant difficulty, intervention recommended |

### Pattern Analysis

Look for patterns across assessments:
- **Category-specific deficits:** Difficulty limited to one domain
- **Widespread difficulties:** Multiple visual processing areas affected
- **Level effects:** Better at Level 1 than Level 2 (consistent with expectations)
- **Inconsistent performance:** Variable results suggesting attention or fatigue factors

### Contextual Factors

Consider when interpreting results:
- Age and developmental level
- Fatigue and time of day
- Motivation and engagement
- Prior exposure to similar tasks
- Sensory environment during assessment
- Cultural and linguistic factors

### When to Refer

Consider referral for comprehensive evaluation when:
- Significant deficits observed across multiple categories
- Performance well below age expectations
- Functional impact on daily activities
- Sudden onset of difficulties
- Concerns raised by multiple observers
- Family history of vision difficulties

## Integration with Other Assessments

Percepto results should be considered alongside:

- **Comprehensive Eye Exam:** Visual acuity, refraction, eye health
- **Neuropsychological Evaluation:** Cognitive factors affecting performance
- **Occupational Therapy Assessment:** Functional vision in daily activities
- **Educational Assessment:** Academic impact of visual difficulties
- **Medical Evaluation:** Rule out neurological or systemic conditions

## Documentation and Reporting

### Clinical Notes Should Include:
1. Assessment context (date, time, environment)
2. Patient status (fatigue, motivation, health)
3. Quantitative results (scores, times)
4. Qualitative observations (behaviors, strategies)
5. Pattern analysis across categories
6. Functional implications
7. Recommendations

### Report Structure:
1. **Referral Information:** Reason for assessment
2. **Background:** Relevant history
3. **Assessment Battery:** What was administered
4. **Results:** Category-by-category results
5. **Interpretation:** Clinical meaning of findings
6. **Recommendations:** Interventions and accommodations
7. **Prognosis:** Expected outcomes with support
